EV Charging RFPs Explained: Scope, Funding (NEVI/CFI) & Where to Find Them

Public agencies buy EV charging three main ways: Level 2 chargers for workplaces, fleets and public lots, DC fast charging (DCFC) sites, or a turnkey / charging-as-a-service contract that bundles design, make-ready electrical work, hardware, network software and operations. Many of these RFPs are driven by federal NEVI (23 CFR 680) or CFI money or by state programs in California, Texas and New York. Below: typical scope, what the rules require, the funding sources with official links, and where to find open bids.

Level 2, DC fast, and how agencies structure the purchase

  • Level 2 (AC): in the NEVI rule an AC Level 2 port must provide at least 6 kW continuous and have a permanently attached J1772 connector (23 CFR 680.106). Typical for workplaces, multifamily, fleets and parking.
  • DC fast charging (DCFC): NEVI DCFC ports must support 250 to 920 volts DC; ports on designated Alternative Fuel Corridors must deliver at least 150 kW continuous, simultaneously from each port (680.106(d)).
  • Turnkey vs charging-as-a-service: in a turnkey RFP the agency or site owner buys a built station. In a charging-as-a-service or operator model a private operator installs, owns or runs the chargers and the agency provides the site (a site host). The notice states who owns the equipment, who sets prices and who pays for utility service.

Typical scope in an EV charging RFP

  1. Site assessment and design: parking layout, electrical capacity, utility service and ADA access.
  2. Make-ready: trenching, conduit, transformers, switchgear and the utility interconnection or service upgrade.
  3. Hardware: chargers, connectors and payment equipment to a stated spec.
  4. Networking and software: for NEVI projects 23 CFR 680.108 requires chargers to conform to OCPP 1.6J or higher, and OCPP 2.0.1 by February 28, 2024, plus ISO 15118 charger-to-vehicle communication.
  5. Operations, maintenance and uptime: 680.116(b) requires each NEVI charging port to average more than 97% annual uptime, calculated monthly over the previous twelve months, with defined exclusions such as utility outages and scheduled maintenance.
  6. Data reporting: NEVI recipients must report charging-session and uptime data and make station data available to third parties through an API (680.116).

What 23 CFR 680 (NEVI) requires

Official text: eCFR, 23 CFR part 680 (we read it on November 21, 2026; eCFR shows no amendments to the part since March 30, 2023). Key points that show up in RFP specs:

  • Four ports (680.106(b)): a station must have at least four network-connected charging ports able to charge four EVs at once. Along designated Alternative Fuel Corridors those must be DCFC ports rated at 150 kW or more each, which is where the common “4 x 150 kW” shorthand comes from.
  • Connectors (680.106(c)): each DCFC port must charge any CCS-compliant vehicle and have a permanently attached CCS Type 1 connector.
  • Availability (680.106(e)): stations along Alternative Fuel Corridors must be accessible 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.
  • Payment (680.106(f)): a contactless card payment method and no membership requirement, unless charging is free.
  • Qualified technicians (680.106(j)): installers must meet EVITP certification or registered electrical apprenticeship requirements stated in the rule.
  • Procurement transparency (680.106(a)): states must make public a summary of the procurement process, the number of bids received, the awardee, the proposed contract and a financial summary.
  • Uptime (680.116(b)): more than 97% average annual uptime per port.
  • Buy America and wages (680.118): Buy America requirements at 23 U.S.C. 313 and the Build America, Buy America Act apply, and Davis-Bacon federal wage rates must be paid on projects funded with NEVI Formula Program funds.

Program administration has also moved since the rule: FHWA’s NEVI page links the August 11, 2025 NEVI Formula Program Interim Final Guidance. Read it alongside the rule and your state’s current solicitation.

Funding sources, with official links

  • NEVI (federal formula): funds go to state DOTs, which run their own solicitations. TxDOT states Texas will receive $323 million for fiscal years 2022-2026, split 80 percent federal and 20 percent state, with site developers paying the state share. TxDOT reports $226 million awarded for 321 stations in Phase II (264 in metropolitan planning organization areas, 57 in county seats). NYSERDA states New York will receive approximately $175 million through NEVI over five years; in April 2026 it announced a $45 million AFC and Community NEVI DCFC program (PON 6150, proposals were due June 23, 2026).
  • CFI (federal discretionary grants): per the FHWA CFI page, the program is codified at 23 U.S.C. §151, provides $2.5 billion over five years across a Community Program and a Corridor Program, and eligible applicants include states, local governments, MPOs, special-purpose districts and tribes. Round 1A, 1B and 2 recipients are listed; future rounds will be announced on Grants.gov. NYSERDA says it was awarded $15 million in the first CFI round.
  • California: CALeVIP is funded through the California Energy Commission’s Clean Transportation Program. CALeVIP 2.0 funds DC fast chargers only and directs 50% of overall project funding to low-income and disadvantaged communities. CALeVIP’s site says the Fast Charge California Project Window 2 is now accepting applications.
  • Texas: TxDOT’s EV planning page lists the FY2026 Texas EV Plan and says Phase 1 construction began for 25 charging sites, with 45 more stations along Alternative Fuel Corridors in planning.
  • New York: Charge Ready NY 2.0 offers rebates for Level 2 stations at workplaces, multifamily properties and hotels: $3,000 per port, plus $1,000 per port in a disadvantaged community. EVolve NY (New York Power Authority) is installing up to 400 fast-charging stations statewide by early 2027.

Figures above are as stated on the linked official pages on November 21, 2026. Program rules and open windows change; confirm on the source before bidding.

Ownership and pricing models

Read the RFP for four decisions: who owns the chargers (agency, operator or utility), who operates and maintains them, who sets the price to drivers, and who pays for electricity and demand charges. NEVI also constrains pricing display: 23 CFR 680.116(a) requires the price to be shown before a session starts, based on $/kWh, and not to change during the session. Agencies may pay a fixed price for installation, a per-port or per-session fee, or share revenue with an operator; the solicitation says which.

Evaluation criteria to expect

EV charging RFPs are usually best-value, not lowest price. Common criteria are price (capital cost or revenue share), technical approach and site design, experience delivering comparable stations, uptime and maintenance commitments, schedule, equipment compliance (for NEVI: standards above, Buy America), and qualified-workforce commitments. Construction-only invitations to bid are more often lowest responsive bid.

FAQ

What do public agencies buy in an EV charging RFP?

Usually one of three things: Level 2 chargers for workplaces, fleets and public lots; DC fast charging (DCFC) sites; or a bundled turnkey or charging-as-a-service contract that covers design, make-ready electrical work, hardware, network software and multi-year operations and maintenance. The notice states which, and whether the agency owns the chargers or a private operator does.

What does NEVI require of a funded charging station?

Under 23 CFR 680, a NEVI-funded station must have at least four network-connected charging ports able to charge four EVs at once. DC fast ports along designated Alternative Fuel Corridors must deliver at least 150 kW per port simultaneously, each port must average more than 97% annual uptime, and chargers must meet the rule's connector, payment, data-reporting, OCPP and qualified-technician requirements. Buy America and Davis-Bacon wage rules apply to NEVI-funded work.

What is the difference between NEVI and CFI?

NEVI is a formula program: federal money flows to state DOTs, which run their own competitive solicitations. CFI (Charging and Fueling Infrastructure) is a discretionary grant program codified at 23 U.S.C. 151 where states, local governments, MPOs, tribes and other eligible applicants apply directly to FHWA when a notice of funding opportunity is open.

Who pays for the chargers in a NEVI project?

It depends on the state. TxDOT says Texas NEVI funds are split 80 percent federal and 20 percent state share, with site developers paying the state share. Other states structure awards differently, so read each solicitation.
