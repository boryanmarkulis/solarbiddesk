ESPC vs PPA: Energy Savings Performance Contracts vs Solar PPAs for Public Agencies

In an ESPC the agency owns upgrades paid from guaranteed energy savings; in a PPA a developer owns the solar and sells the power. ESPCs bundle efficiency work with solar and carry an ESCO savings guarantee. PPAs need no capital and price power per kWh. As of November 28, 2026 we track 4 open public solar bids.

How an ESPC works

The U.S. Department of Energy describes a performance contract as an agreement in which the contractor identifies, designs and installs energy conservation measures and guarantees their performance. Financing is often arranged by the contractor, and terms commonly run 5 to 10 years for simple projects and 20 years or more for larger ones (DOE). DOE also notes that ESPC is not a financing source on its own, and that solar can often be integrated depending on state rules (DOE ESPC blueprint).

State law sets the frame. Texas Local Government Code chapter 302 defines an ESPC as a contract whose estimated savings are subject to guarantee to offset the cost of the measures over a set period (LGC ch. 302). California Gov Code 4217.12 lets a public agency sign an energy service contract after a noticed public hearing and a cost finding (Gov Code 4217.12); see our California school solar and 4217 guide.

How a solar PPA works for a public agency

A developer designs, builds, owns and operates the system on agency property and sells the output at a set rate, often with an escalator. The agency needs no capital, but signs a long site lease and a long power contract. Evaluation leans on price per kWh, term, escalator, buyout and removal terms. Full detail: the solar PPA RFP guide.

Tax timing matters more since 2025. Solar placed in service after December 31, 2027 loses the 48E credit unless construction began by July 4, 2026, and that flows into PPA prices. Read the elective pay guide.

Which one should a bidder chase?

  • ESCOs and design-build firms fit ESPCs: qualifications, audits and measurement and verification capacity win them.
  • Developers with capital or tax equity fit PPAs: the price per kWh wins them.
  • Installers and EPCs can subcontract to either. Find the prime early and offer firm solar pricing.
  • Storage: both models can include batteries, and storage stays credit-eligible after the solar cutoff.

FAQ

What is the difference between an ESPC and a PPA?

In an ESPC the agency owns the installed measures and pays for them from guaranteed energy and operating savings, with the ESCO covering shortfalls. In a PPA a developer owns and operates the solar system and the agency buys the power at a set price per kWh.

Can solar be part of an energy savings performance contract?

Often, yes. DOE notes that distributed renewables such as solar can often be integrated into ESPC projects depending on state regulations. The savings from all measures together must support the financing.

Which is better for a school district: ESPC or PPA?

It depends on capital, risk appetite and scope. An ESPC suits a district that wants to own equipment and upgrade many buildings at once; a PPA suits one that wants solar with no capital. In California both can run as Gov Code 4217 energy service contracts. Get independent advice.

What is an ESCO?

An energy service company. DOE describes it as a turnkey contractor that designs, installs and commissions the project, measures and verifies the savings and guarantees them, and in some cases maintains the equipment.
