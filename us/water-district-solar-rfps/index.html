How water and wastewater districts buy solar

According to the U.S. EPA, drinking water and wastewater plants are typically the largest energy consumers for many municipal governments, often 30 to 40 percent of the total, and as much as 40 percent of operating costs for drinking water systems can be energy. EPA lists solar panels as the most common alternative energy source for utilities, and says a utility can buy and operate its own generation equipment or contract with a third-party provider that owns and manages it on-site (EPA, Energy Efficiency for Water Utilities, read November 25, 2026). In practice that gives three procurement routes:

  • District-owned (RFP, EPC or design-build): the district pays to build the system and keeps the output. The RFP scores price or lifecycle cost, technical approach, experience and schedule.
  • Third-party owned (PPA): a developer owns and operates the system and the district buys the energy, usually with a site lease. See our solar PPA RFP guide.
  • California energy service contract (Gov Code 4217): see below.

PPAs and California Gov Code 4217

California Government Code 4217.11 defines “public agency” to include a district, community college district, school district and joint powers authority, and defines an “energy service contract” as a contract under which a person provides electrical or thermal energy or conservation services to the agency from an energy conservation facility (Gov Code 4217.11). Under Gov Code 4217.12, the governing body may enter an energy service contract and any necessarily related facility ground lease on terms it determines are in the public agency’s best interests, if it decides at a regularly scheduled public hearing, with public notice at least two weeks in advance, and finds that the anticipated cost of the energy or conservation services will be less than the anticipated marginal cost of the energy it would otherwise have consumed. This is general information, not legal advice. For the school-district version of the same rules, see our California school solar RFP guide.

Net metering aggregation and bill-credit transfer

Districts often have many meters (pump stations, wells, plants, offices). The CPUC describes NEM aggregation, authorized by SB 594, as letting an eligible customer-generator aggregate the load of multiple meters, with credits shared among property that is attached, adjacent or contiguous to the generation facility. It also describes RES-BCT, a tariff that lets local governments and universities share generation credits from a system on one government-owned property with billing accounts at other government-owned properties, with a 5 MW system size limit (CPUC, Net Energy Metering, read November 25, 2026). Rules differ by utility and change over time, so confirm current eligibility before pricing a bid.

Solar + storage for pump-station resilience

Critical pumping and treatment loads are the usual reason a water district adds batteries or a microgrid controller to a solar project: keeping service up during an outage. The RFP then adds a critical-load list, hours of backup required and islanding or transfer-switch design. See our solar + storage and microgrid RFP hub.

Floating solar

Some districts consider floating solar on reservoirs or treatment ponds where land is scarce. It is an emerging option that adds anchoring, water-level and cable requirements; we cite no figures for it here. If a district issues such an RFP, it will be in the notice.

What a water district solar RFP asks for

  1. Site and load review: meters, pump stations, treatment plant, reservoir or land available, and any critical loads.
  2. Design and interconnection: single-line diagram, utility interconnection application and approval, tariff or NEM aggregation approach.
  3. Equipment and installation: PV, inverters, optional storage, to the stated specification, with commissioning and acceptance testing.
  4. Operations and monitoring: multi-year O&M, performance reporting or guarantees, often priced separately.
  5. Contract terms: bonding, insurance, prevailing wage and ownership model (owned, PPA or energy service contract). The notice states these.
  6. Proposal contents: experience on comparable public projects, references, team, schedule, pricing form and any required district forms.

Where water districts post bids

  • PlanetBids and BidNet: common vendor portals for California agencies, including special districts.
  • New York State Contract Reporter (NYSCR): New York state and authority solicitations.
  • Texas ESBD: the Texas Electronic State Business Daily for state-level bids.
  • The district’s own site and board agenda, and SAM.gov for federal notices.

FAQ

Are there open water district solar RFPs right now?

Not in our verified feed as of November 25, 2026. We check PlanetBids, Texas ESBD, NY Contract Reporter, Caltrans and SAM.gov daily; join the free weekly digest or check the solar RFP hub for new notices.

How do water and wastewater districts buy solar?

Usually one of three ways: an ordinary RFP or bid for design and construction (EPC or design-build) where the district owns the system; a power purchase agreement where a developer owns and operates it and the district buys the energy; or, in California, an energy service contract under Government Code 4217.10-4217.18. The EPA notes utilities can buy and operate their own renewable generation or contract with a third-party provider that owns and manages it on-site. The solicitation states which route applies.

Why are water and wastewater plants a big solar buyer segment?

Energy is a large cost for them. EPA states that drinking water and wastewater plants are typically among the largest energy consumers for municipal governments, often 30 to 40 percent of the total, and that as much as 40 percent of operating costs for drinking water systems can be energy. EPA also lists solar panels as the most common on-site alternative energy source for utilities.

Does California Gov Code 4217 apply to water districts?

The statute defines "public agency" to include a district, a community college district, a school district, a joint powers authority and other political subdivisions (Gov Code 4217.11). Under 4217.12 the governing body may enter an energy service contract and a related facility ground lease if it decides at a regularly scheduled public hearing, with at least two weeks' public notice, and finds the anticipated cost will be below the marginal cost of the energy it would otherwise use. This is general information, not legal advice; confirm the route with the district's counsel.

What is NEM aggregation and does it help a water district?

According to the CPUC, net energy metering aggregation lets an eligible customer-generator combine the load of multiple meters, with bill credits shared among properties that are attached, adjacent or contiguous to the generation facility, which can suit a district with several metered accounts on neighboring parcels. The CPUC also describes the RES-BCT tariff, which lets local governments share generation credits from a system on one government-owned property with accounts at other government-owned properties, with a 5 MW system size limit. Eligibility and current tariff rules depend on the utility, so check them before bidding.

What does a water district solar RFP usually ask for?

Expect a site and load review (pump stations, treatment plants, reservoirs), system design, utility interconnection, equipment to stated standards, installation and commissioning, monitoring and a multi-year O&M term. Where the district wants outage resilience for critical pumping, the scope may add batteries and controls. Public works rules such as bonding and prevailing wage often apply; the notice states which.

What about floating solar on reservoirs and wastewater ponds?

Floating solar is an emerging option for some man-made water bodies, and an RFP for it typically adds anchoring, water-level and cable design requirements. It is far less common in U.S. public solicitations than rooftop, carport or ground-mount, so we describe it here only in general terms and cite no figures. Read the notice for what the district actually wants.
