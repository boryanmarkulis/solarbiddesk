How public agencies buy solar carports

Schools, cities, transit agencies and special districts (including water districts) often have large parking lots and limited roof space, which is why carports and parking canopies come up in solar procurement. The route is set in the solicitation. In general there are three:

  • Design-build or EPC RFP (agency-owned): the agency pays to build the canopy and keeps the output and any incentives it qualifies for. The RFP usually scores price or lifecycle cost, technical approach, experience and schedule.
  • PPA or lease (third-party owned): a developer finances, owns and operates the system and the agency buys the energy, usually with a site lease. See our solar PPA RFP guide.
  • California energy service contract (Gov Code 4217): see below.

California Gov Code 4217 and cooperative contracts

California Government Code 4217.11 defines “public agency” to include a district, community college district, school district and joint powers authority, and defines an “energy service contract” as a contract under which a person provides electrical or thermal energy or conservation services to the agency from an energy conservation facility (Gov Code 4217.11). Under Gov Code 4217.12, the governing body may enter an energy service contract and any necessarily related facility ground lease if it decides at a regularly scheduled public hearing, with public notice at least two weeks in advance, and finds that the anticipated cost will be less than the anticipated marginal cost of the energy it would otherwise have consumed. This is general information, not legal advice. For the school-district version, see our California school solar RFP guide.

Some agencies instead buy through a cooperative contract, one that another public agency competitively awarded and that other agencies may be allowed to use. Whether that is permitted, and for what scope, depends on the buyer’s rules and the original contract, so confirm both.

EV-ready canopies

A parking canopy is a natural place to add EV charging. An “EV-ready” scope typically asks for conduit, electrical capacity and mounting provisions so chargers can be added now or later; some notices include the chargers, others bid them separately. See our EV charging RFP hub and the EV charging RFP guide.

What a solar carport RFP asks for

  1. Site and parking layout: lot boundaries, stalls to be covered, traffic flow, and loads to be served.
  2. Structural and geotechnical: canopy design, foundations, soil investigation, wind and snow loads per the local code, and drainage.
  3. Clearances and safety: ADA access, fire-lane and vehicle height clearances, and lighting under the canopy.
  4. Electrical and EV: PV, inverters, utility interconnection, and conduit and capacity for EV charging.
  5. Operations: commissioning, monitoring and a multi-year O&M term, often priced separately.
  6. Contract terms: bonding, insurance, prevailing wage and the ownership model (owned, PPA or energy service contract). The notice states these.
  7. Proposal contents: experience on comparable public projects, references, team, schedule, pricing form and any required agency forms.

Where agencies post carport bids

  • PlanetBids and BidNet: common vendor portals for California agencies, including schools and special districts.
  • New York State Contract Reporter (NYSCR): New York state and authority solicitations.
  • Texas ESBD: the Texas Electronic State Business Daily for state-level bids.
  • The agency’s own site and board agenda.

How do public agencies buy solar carports?

Usually one of three ways: a design-build or EPC RFP where the agency owns the structure and the solar; a power purchase agreement or lease where a developer owns and operates it and the agency buys the energy; or, in California, an energy service contract under Government Code 4217.10-4217.18. The solicitation states which route applies, and some agencies buy through an existing cooperative contract instead of a new RFP.

Is a solar carport RFP different from a rooftop solar RFP?

Often yes. A carport is a structure as well as a power plant, so the RFP typically adds structural and geotechnical design, foundations, drainage, lighting, and clearances for vehicles, fire lanes and accessibility. Costs per watt are usually higher than rooftop or ground-mount for that reason, though the agency may value the shade and the covered parking. Read each notice for its exact scope.

Does California Gov Code 4217 apply to carport projects?

The statute defines "public agency" to include a district, a community college district, a school district, a joint powers authority and other political subdivisions (Gov Code 4217.11). Under 4217.12 the governing body may enter an energy service contract and a related facility ground lease if it decides at a regularly scheduled public hearing, with at least two weeks' public notice, and finds the anticipated cost will be below the marginal cost of the energy it would otherwise use. Whether a given carport project fits is a question for the agency's counsel; this is general information, not legal advice.

What is a cooperative contract and can it be used for carports?

A cooperative (or piggyback) contract is a contract one public agency competitively awarded that other agencies may use under their own rules, so they can skip running a separate RFP. Some agencies buy solar or canopy work this way. Whether it is allowed, and for which scope, depends on the buyer's procurement rules and the original contract's terms, so confirm both before relying on it.

What does an EV-ready solar canopy mean in an RFP?

It usually means the carport design includes conduit, panel capacity and pads or mounting points so EV chargers can be added now or later, and sometimes the chargers themselves. Some notices bundle chargers into the same scope; others keep them as a separate bid. See our EV charging RFP hub and EV charging RFP guide for how those bids are written.

What does a solar carport RFP usually ask for?

Expect a site and parking-layout review, structural and geotechnical design, foundations, PV and inverters, lighting and conduit, utility interconnection, ADA and fire-lane clearances, optional EV-charging readiness, commissioning and a multi-year O&M term. Public works rules such as bonding and prevailing wage often apply; the notice states which.
